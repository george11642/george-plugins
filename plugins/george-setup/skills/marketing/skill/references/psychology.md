## Top 20 Most Actionable Models

### 1. Loss Aversion
Losses feel 2x as painful as equivalent gains. Frame in terms of what they'll lose by not acting. "Don't miss out" beats "You could gain."

### 2. Anchoring Effect
First number seen heavily influences judgments. Show higher price first (original price, competitor price, enterprise tier) to anchor expectations.

### 3. Social Proof / Bandwagon
People follow what others do. Show customer counts, testimonials, logos, reviews, "trending" indicators.

### 4. Reciprocity
People feel obligated to return favors. Free content, free tools, generous free tiers create reciprocal obligation.

### 5. Scarcity / Urgency
Limited availability increases perceived value. Limited-time offers, low-stock warnings, exclusive access. Only use when genuine.

### 6. Endowment Effect
People value things more once they own them. Free trials and freemium let customers "own" the product, making them reluctant to give it up.

### 7. Commitment & Consistency
Once people commit, they want to stay consistent. Get small commitments first (email signup, free trial) -- people who take one step are more likely to take the next.

### 8. Framing Effect
How something is presented changes perception. "90% success rate" vs "10% failure rate" -- same facts, different impact. Frame positively.

### 9. Paradox of Choice
Too many options overwhelm. Three pricing tiers beat seven. Recommend a "best for most" option.

### 10. Default Effect
People accept pre-selected options. Pre-select the plan you want chosen. Opt-out beats opt-in (ethically applied).

### 11. Goal-Gradient Effect
People accelerate as they approach a goal. Progress bars, completion percentages, "almost there" messaging drive completion.

### 12. Zero-Price Effect
Free is psychologically different from any price. The jump from $1 to $0 is bigger than $2 to $1. Free tiers and shipping have disproportionate appeal.

### 13. IKEA Effect
People value what they've built. Let customers customize, configure, or create. Their investment increases commitment.

### 14. Mere Exposure Effect
Familiarity breeds liking. Consistent brand presence across channels builds preference over time.

### 15. Decoy Effect
Adding an inferior third option makes one of the originals look better. A "decoy" pricing tier makes your preferred tier the obvious choice.

### 16. Authority Bias
People defer to experts. Feature expert endorsements, certifications, "featured in" logos, thought leadership.

### 17. Pratfall Effect
Small flaws make competent brands more likable. Admitting a weakness ("We're not the cheapest, but...") increases trust.

### 18. Status-Quo Bias
People prefer the current state. Reduce friction to switch. "Import your data in one click." Make transition feel safe.

### 19. Hyperbolic Discounting
People prefer immediate rewards. Emphasize "Start saving time today" over "ROI in 6 months."

### 20. Zeigarnik Effect
Unfinished tasks occupy the mind. "You're 80% done" creates pull. Incomplete profiles and abandoned carts leverage this.

## Pricing Psychology

| Principle | Rule | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| Charm Pricing | $99 feels much cheaper than $100 (left-digit effect) | Value-focused products |
| Round Pricing | $100 signals premium, easier to process | Premium products |
| Rule of 100 | Under $100: use % off; Over $100: use $ off | Discounts |
| Good-Better-Best | 3 tiers; middle is target; expensive makes it reasonable | Pricing pages |
| Mental Accounting | "$3/day" feels cheaper than "$90/month" | Reframing cost |
| Door-in-the-Face | Show enterprise price first, then starter plan | Making plans feel affordable |

## Persuasion Frameworks

### BJ Fogg Behavior Model
**Behavior = Motivation x Ability x Prompt.** All three must be present.
- High motivation but hard to do = won't happen
- Easy but no prompt = won't happen
- Design for all three simultaneously

### EAST Framework
Make desired behaviors: **Easy** (reduce friction), **Attractive** (make it appealing), **Social** (show others doing it), **Timely** (ask at the right moment).

### AIDA Funnel
**Attention --> Interest --> Desire --> Action.** Structure pages and campaigns to move through each stage sequentially.

### Foot-in-the-Door
Start small, then escalate. Free trial --> paid --> annual --> enterprise. Each step builds on the last.

### Rule of 7
Prospects need ~7 touchpoints before converting. Build multi-touch campaigns; retargeting, email, and consistent presence compound.

## Strategic Thinking Models

| Model | One-Line Summary | Marketing Application |
|---|---|---|
| First Principles | Break to basic truths, build up | Don't copy competitors -- ask why you need each tactic |
| Jobs to Be Done | People hire products for outcomes | Frame around the job, not specifications |
| Pareto (80/20) | 80% of results from 20% of effort | Find the 20% of channels driving 80% of results |
| Inversion | Ask "what guarantees failure?" and avoid it | List everything that would tank your campaign, then prevent each |
| Theory of Constraints | One bottleneck limits the system | Fix the bottleneck before optimizing elsewhere |
| Opportunity Cost | Every choice costs alternatives | Time on low-ROI channels = time not on high-ROI |
| Second-Order Thinking | Effects of effects | Flash sale boosts revenue but may train discount-waiting |
| Barbell Strategy | 80% safe + 20% high-risk bets | Proven channels + experimental bets; avoid mediocre middle |
| Exploration vs Exploitation | Balance new vs optimizing known | Don't abandon working channels for shiny new ones |

## Growth Models

| Model | Application |
|---|---|
| Feedback Loops | More users --> more content --> better SEO --> more users. Strengthen positive loops. |
| Compounding | Consistent content/SEO/brand compounds exponentially. Start early. |
| Network Effects | Features that improve with more users: shared workspaces, communities. |
| Flywheel | Content --> traffic --> leads --> customers --> case studies --> more content. |
| Switching Costs | Increase ethically: integrations, data accumulation, team adoption. |
| Critical Mass | Focus on one segment before expanding. Depth before breadth. |
| Survivorship Bias | Study failed campaigns too. The viral hit had 99 invisible failures. |
