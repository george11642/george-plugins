# Research Ethics: Frameworks, IRB, and Decolonial Methodology

## Ethical Frameworks for Research

Research ethics draws on multiple philosophical traditions. Understanding which framework applies to a given situation helps when navigating dilemmas that rules alone cannot resolve.

### Consequentialism (Utilitarian Ethics)
- **Core principle**: Actions are right if they maximize benefit and minimize harm to the greatest number
- **Application**: Risk-benefit analysis in IRB review; justifying invasive procedures by anticipated knowledge gain
- **Strengths**: Clear decision calculus; addresses aggregate welfare
- **Weaknesses**: Can justify harming individuals for collective benefit (Tuskegee was defended on these grounds); difficult to quantify or compare harms

### Deontological Ethics (Kant, Belmont Report)
- **Core principle**: Certain acts are intrinsically right or wrong regardless of consequences; persons must always be treated as ends, never merely as means
- **The Belmont Principles** (US biomedical standard):
  - **Respect for persons**: Autonomy must be honored; informed consent is required
  - **Beneficence**: Maximize benefits, minimize risks
  - **Justice**: Equitable distribution of research burdens and benefits
- **Application**: Consent requirements, confidentiality obligations, prohibitions on deception without debriefing
- **Strengths**: Protects individual rights absolutely
- **Weaknesses**: Can be rigid; conflicts between principles are common

### Virtue Ethics
- **Core principle**: Ethics is about character — what would a virtuous researcher do?
- **Application**: Research integrity (honesty, transparency, humility); mentorship and power relations; responding to unexpected findings
- **Relevant virtues**: Intellectual honesty, epistemic humility, care for participants, courage to report negative results
- **Strengths**: Addresses relational and character aspects that rules miss
- **Weaknesses**: No clear decision procedure; culturally variable notions of virtue

### Feminist and Care Ethics
- **Core principle**: Relationships, context, and care are morally primary; abstract principles miss the relational nature of ethical situations
- **Application**: Participatory and community-based research; attention to power differentials; avoiding extractive research
- **Strengths**: Centers voice of marginalized groups; attentive to power
- **Weaknesses**: Can be difficult to generalize; risk of researcher paternalism

## IRB / Research Ethics Board Review

### When Full vs Expedited vs Exempt Review Applies

**Exempt review** (no ongoing IRB oversight required):
- Research involving educational tests, surveys, or observations where disclosure cannot harm subjects
- Secondary analysis of existing, deidentified data
- Benign behavioral interventions with normal educational practices
- Note: Exempt does NOT mean unethical — you still must assess risks and protect participants

**Expedited review** (reviewed by IRB chair or designated member):
- Research involving no more than minimal risk
- Minor medical procedures (blood draw ≤550mL, skin biopsy)
- Studies of individual or group characteristics involving voice, video, digital data
- Research on existing data that is not public
- Most survey and interview studies with adults

**Full board review** (convened IRB required):
- Greater than minimal risk
- Vulnerable populations (see below)
- Deception studies
- Biomedical studies with medical procedures or drugs
- Research involving prisoners, children, pregnant women

### Informed Consent Elements

Per 45 CFR 46 (US Common Rule), valid informed consent requires:

**Competency**: Subject must have decision-making capacity (cognitive ability to understand and reason). Special procedures for children (assent + parental permission) and cognitively impaired adults.

**Voluntariness**: No coercion or undue inducement. Compensation should not be so large as to compromise voluntary choice. Power relationships must be acknowledged (e.g., teacher-student, employer-employee).

**Information**: Must disclose:
1. Purpose of the research and expected duration
2. Foreseeable risks and discomforts
3. Anticipated benefits
4. Alternative procedures or treatments (clinical research)
5. Confidentiality protections
6. Voluntary participation and right to withdraw without penalty
7. Contact information for questions

### Vulnerable Populations

**Children**: Parental/guardian permission + child assent (7+ years; may be waived for minor risk or if parental involvement would harm). Institutional policies vary.

**Prisoners**: Additional protections (45 CFR Part 46 Subpart C). Must demonstrate that research cannot be done outside prison; prison population may not receive disproportionate research burden.

**Pregnant women, fetuses, and neonates**: Additional protections (Subpart B). Research must present minimal risk or direct benefit.

**Cognitively impaired adults**: Surrogate consent may be permitted; assent from the individual still sought when possible; re-consent as cognition changes.

**Students, employees**: Power differential creates coercion risk. Participation must have no effect on grades, employment, or standing. Alternative activities must be available.

### Ongoing Consent in Longitudinal Studies

- Re-consent required when: study scope changes, new risks emerge, significant time has passed
- Participants retain right to withdraw at any time without penalty or loss of benefits
- Data collected before withdrawal typically remains in dataset (specify in original consent)
- Consider "rolling consent" for ecological momentary assessment or longitudinal digital data

## Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR)

### Core CBPR Principles (Israel et al., 1998)

1. **Recognizes community as a unit of identity** (not just a collection of individuals)
2. **Builds on community strengths and resources**
3. **Facilitates collaborative, equitable partnership** in all research phases
4. **Promotes co-learning and capacity building** among all partners
5. **Integrates and achieves balance** between research and action for the benefit of all partners
6. **Emphasizes local relevance** of public health problems
7. **Involves systems development using a cyclical and iterative process**
8. **Disseminates findings and knowledge gained to all partners**
9. **Involves long-term commitment by all partners**

### Practical CBPR Structures

**Community Advisory Boards (CABs)**:
- Composed of community members, organizational leaders, stakeholders
- Formally involved in study design, protocol review, interpretation
- Compensated for their expertise and time
- Different from IRBs — not regulatory, but collaborative

**Benefit-sharing agreements**:
- Specify how findings will benefit the community, not just the researchers
- May include: job training, data ownership, authorship, co-presentation
- Establish before study begins; document in writing

**Co-investigator and co-author roles**: Community partners as co-PIs on grants; authorship credit per CRediT taxonomy contributions (see Research Integrity section).

### CBPR for Indigenous Communities: OCAP Principles

The OCAP framework (First Nations Information Governance Centre, Canada) asserts:

- **Ownership**: The community collectively owns its cultural knowledge, data, stories. Data collected from community members belongs to the community.
- **Control**: The community has authority to control all aspects of research that affect them — from conception through dissemination.
- **Access**: Community members have the right to access their own information and manage how it is used.
- **Possession**: Physical control of data as practical expression of ownership. Data should be held by the community, not in researcher's institution.

**CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Carroll et al., 2020):
- **Collective Benefit**: Data ecosystems should enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit
- **Authority to Control**: Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in data must be recognized
- **Responsibility**: Data stewards have responsibilities to people from whom data were collected
- **Ethics**: Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should be central throughout the data life cycle

**Implications for research design**:
- Community consultation before study design begins
- Tribal resolutions or community agreements as formal approval
- Local ethics review in addition to university IRB
- Repatriation of data and findings to community
- Community veto rights on publication

## Decolonial Methodologies

Decolonial methodology challenges the assumption that Western academic research methods are universal, objective, or neutral. It asks: *whose knowledge counts, who benefits from research, and what epistemological frameworks are being privileged?*

### Key Critiques of Mainstream Research

- Positivism's claim to universal objectivity erases indigenous and local ways of knowing
- Extractive research: researchers take knowledge, leave nothing behind
- Epistemic injustice: systematically ignoring or dismissing knowledge produced by marginalized groups
- Colonial categories embedded in measurement instruments (DSM diagnoses, IQ tests, development indices)

### Core Commitments of Decolonial Research

**Centering marginalized epistemologies**: Treat non-Western knowledge traditions as legitimate sources, not just data. Ubuntu (I am because we are), Indigenous relational ontologies, and Southern theory (Connell, 2007) offer alternative analytical frameworks.

**Researcher positionality**: Explicitly examine how your social location (race, class, gender, nationality, institutional affiliation) shapes your research questions, interpretations, and relationships with participants.

**Research as transformation (not just description)**: Knowledge should serve liberation, not merely describe oppression. Praxis: theory connected to action.

**Decolonizing the literature review**: Include sources from Global South journals; cite indigenous scholars; resist the assumption that knowledge only becomes legitimate after appearing in high-impact Western journals.

### Reflexivity About Researcher Positionality

A positionality statement (increasingly required in qualitative journals) should address:
1. Your identity in relation to the research community (insider, outsider, or somewhere between)
2. Power differentials between you and participants
3. How your assumptions may have shaped research design and interpretation
4. What you did to mitigate bias from your positionality

### Ethical Protocols for Cross-Cultural Research

- Research permits from relevant national/local authorities in addition to home institution IRB
- Local co-investigators with genuine intellectual partnership (not just "fixers")
- Language and cultural competency — avoid translation artifacts in instruments
- Return of findings in accessible formats (local language, community meetings, not just journal publications)
- Consider whether the research question was identified by the community or imported by the researcher

## Research Integrity

### Authorship: CRediT Taxonomy

The Contributor Roles Taxonomy (CRediT) provides 14 defined contributor roles to replace vague "authorship" claims:

| Role | Description |
|---|---|
| Conceptualization | Ideas, formulation, evolution of overarching research goals |
| Data Curation | Management activities to annotate, maintain, and produce clean data |
| Formal Analysis | Application of statistical, mathematical, or computational techniques |
| Funding Acquisition | Acquisition of financial support for the project |
| Investigation | Conducting data collection or experiments |
| Methodology | Development or design of methodology |
| Project Administration | Management of research activity planning and execution |
| Resources | Provision of study materials, reagents, samples, instruments |
| Software | Programming, software development, implementation |
| Supervision | Oversight and leadership responsibility |
| Validation | Verification of results/experiments |
| Visualization | Preparation, creation, or presentation of published work |
| Writing — original draft | Preparation of the published work |
| Writing — review & editing | Critical review, commentary, or revision |

**Authorship criteria (ICMJE)**: To be listed as author, a contributor must have: (1) substantial contribution to conception/design or acquisition/analysis/interpretation; AND (2) drafted or critically revised the work; AND (3) approved the final version; AND (4) accountable for all aspects. All four criteria must be met.

### Conflicts of Interest

- Disclose all financial relationships with entities that may benefit from the research
- Disclose prior public positions that could bias interpretation
- Funding sources disclosed in acknowledgments; role of funder in study design stated
- Clinical trial result reporting independent of sponsor influence (ICMJE guidelines)

### Research Misconduct

**Fabrication**: Making up data or results and recording or reporting them.

**Falsification**: Manipulating research materials, equipment, processes, or changing/omitting data so findings do not accurately represent the research record.

**Plagiarism**: Appropriating another's ideas, processes, results, or words without attribution.

**Questionable Research Practices (QRPs)** — not misconduct but harmful:
- HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known)
- p-hacking / selective reporting
- Selective exclusion of outliers to improve results
- Duplicate publication without disclosure

### Retraction and Correction Procedures

- **Correction (erratum)**: Minor errors that do not affect conclusions
- **Retraction**: Major errors that invalidate conclusions, or misconduct discovered
- Retraction Watch (retractionwatch.com): Database of retracted papers; check before citing old papers in active areas

## Data Privacy in Research

### GDPR Implications for Research Data

The EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies to research involving EU residents:
- **Lawful basis for processing**: Research typically uses "legitimate interests" or "public task" basis
- **Data minimization**: Collect only what is necessary for stated research purposes
- **Purpose limitation**: Data collected for one study cannot be repurposed without consent or new legal basis
- **Storage limitation**: Data should not be kept longer than necessary (define retention period in DMP)
- **Special category data**: Health, racial/ethnic origin, political opinions, biometric data require additional safeguards

### Data Minimization in Study Design

Design your study to collect minimum necessary data:
- Justify each variable collected
- Consider whether existing secondary data could answer your question
- Separate identifying data from research data at collection (link via key file)
- Store key file separately with more restrictive access controls
- Consider whether longitudinal linkage is necessary

### Secure Storage and Access Controls

- Pseudonymization: replace names with codes; store linkage key separately
- Encryption: AES-256 for stored data; TLS for data in transit
- Access controls: role-based access; minimum necessary access principle
- Secure data enclaves for highly sensitive data (health records, genomic data)
- Audit trails for who accessed what data when

### Right to Erasure in Longitudinal Studies

GDPR Article 17 gives individuals the right to request deletion of their data. In longitudinal research:
- Specify in consent how withdrawal will be handled (can you remove already-analyzed data?)
- Clearly state what data will be retained after withdrawal (aggregate data, published results)
- Document procedures for responding to erasure requests
- Consider early anonymization to reduce erasure risk after publication
