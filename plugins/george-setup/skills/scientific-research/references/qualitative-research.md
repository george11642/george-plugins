# Qualitative Research Methods

## When to Use Qualitative Methods

Qualitative methods are appropriate when:
- Research questions concern **meaning, experience, process, or context** rather than frequency or magnitude
- You are exploring a new phenomenon with little prior theory (exploratory/generative research)
- The goal is to understand *how* or *why* rather than *how many* or *how much*
- You need to capture participant perspectives in their own terms
- The phenomenon is complex, contextual, or socially constructed

**Contrasting qualitative vs quantitative**: Qualitative is not simply the absence of numbers. It is a paradigmatic orientation toward subjectivity, interpretation, and thick description. Choose based on your research question, not convenience.

**When NOT to use qualitative methods**:
- You need generalizable prevalence estimates → use surveys or epidemiology
- You need to test a pre-specified causal hypothesis → use experimental or quasi-experimental design
- You have large-n data with measurable outcomes → quantitative or mixed methods

## Choosing Your Qualitative Approach

| Approach | Core Question | Theoretical Roots | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thematic Analysis | What are the patterns of meaning? | Flexible (can be realist or constructivist) | Themes |
| Grounded Theory | What theory explains this phenomenon? | Symbolic interactionism | Substantive theory |
| Phenomenology | What is the lived experience of X? | Phenomenological philosophy | Essence/structure of experience |
| Discourse Analysis | How is language constructing reality? | Post-structuralism, Foucault | Discursive patterns |
| Ethnography | How does culture shape practice here? | Anthropology | Rich contextual account |
| Narrative Inquiry | How do people make sense through story? | Narrative psychology | Story structure and meaning |
| Case Study | What can this particular case illuminate? | Pragmatism | Bounded case knowledge |

The choice is determined by your epistemological position (what you believe knowledge is), your ontological position (what you believe reality is), and your research question.

## Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke Reflexive TA)

Thematic analysis (TA) is the most widely used qualitative method due to its flexibility and accessibility. Braun and Clarke's approach — now explicitly called **Reflexive TA** — foregrounds researcher subjectivity and interpretation as a resource, not a problem.

### The Six Phases

**Phase 1: Familiarization with the data**
- Read and re-read all data (interviews, focus groups, documents)
- Take initial notes on ideas, patterns, observations
- Do not code yet — absorb the data holistically
- Aim: develop intimacy with the dataset

**Phase 2: Generating initial codes**
- Work systematically through data line-by-line
- Code for both semantic content and latent meaning
- Code inclusively — over-coding is better than under-coding at this stage
- Codes are short labels capturing something analytically interesting
- Example: "finding community online" → code: "digital belonging"
- Tools: NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA, or even colored highlighters on printed transcripts

**Phase 3: Searching for themes**
- Collate all coded data extracts
- Sort codes into potential themes — look for clustering and patterning
- Create a visual theme map (pen-and-paper or software mind map)
- A theme captures a **pattern of shared meaning** organized around a central concept
- Themes are NOT just topic summaries — they make an interpretive claim

**Phase 4: Reviewing themes**
- Level 1: Review coded data extracts — does the theme hold together?
- Level 2: Review against the full dataset — does the theme make sense in context?
- Merge, split, discard, or create new themes as needed
- Refine the theme map

**Phase 5: Defining and naming themes**
- Write a detailed definition of each theme's essence
- Name should convey the analytic claim, not just describe content
- Write a short narrative analysis of each theme
- Identify the "story" each theme tells and how themes relate

**Phase 6: Writing the report**
- Weave together analytic narrative and data extracts
- Data extracts should illustrate, not constitute, your analysis
- Avoid "quote-dumping" — each extract needs analytic framing
- Follow convention for your field (APA qualitative paper, or journal-specific)

### Inductive vs Deductive TA

- **Inductive (data-driven)**: Codes and themes emerge from data without prior theoretical framework. Use when exploring a new area.
- **Deductive (theory-driven)**: Analysis starts from an existing framework or research question. Use when testing or extending theory.
- **Hybrid**: Start inductively, then map onto theoretical concepts.

### Semantic vs Latent Themes

- **Semantic themes**: Capture the explicit, surface meaning of what participants said
- **Latent themes**: Go beneath the surface to underlying assumptions, ideologies, or contradictions
- Reflexive TA emphasizes moving toward latent-level interpretation

### Example Theme Map Structure

```
Dataset: interviews with first-generation college students

Theme 1: "Navigating two worlds"
  - Subtheme 1a: Identity tension between home and university
  - Subtheme 1b: Code-switching as survival strategy

Theme 2: "Invisible labor of belonging"
  - Subtheme 2a: Performing competence to avoid stereotypes
  - Subtheme 2b: Emotional cost of perpetual justification

Theme 3: "Finding unexpected allies"
  - Subtheme 3a: Faculty mentorship as transformative
  - Subtheme 3b: Peer networks across shared experience
```

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- Treating TA as mechanical (just following the phases without reflexive engagement)
- Confusing a theme with a topic or category — themes make interpretive claims
- Under-analysis (just describing what participants said)
- Not engaging with all the data
- Calling your approach "TA" without specifying Reflexive TA, IPA, or framework analysis

## Grounded Theory

Grounded theory (GT) aims to generate substantive theory grounded in data. Use when no adequate theory exists for your phenomenon.

**Three major versions**:
- **Classic/Glaserian**: Constant comparison, theoretical sensitivity, avoid prior literature
- **Straussian (Strauss & Corbin)**: More structured coding scheme, paradigm model
- **Constructivist GT (Charmaz)**: Most widely used today; acknowledges researcher co-construction

### Key Procedures

**Theoretical sampling**: You do not decide your sample in advance. You sample based on what emerging theory needs. After early analysis, you identify gaps and purposefully sample to fill them. You stop when concepts are theoretically saturated.

**Constant comparative analysis**: Simultaneously collect, code, and analyze data. Compare incident with incident, incident with code, code with code, to develop theoretical properties of categories.

**Coding stages**:
1. **Open (initial) coding**: Line-by-line coding with gerunds (process-focused labels): "seeking reassurance", "managing uncertainty"
2. **Focused coding**: Use most frequent/significant codes to sift through larger data amounts
3. **Axial coding (Straussian)**: Relate categories to subcategories around conditions, context, actions, consequences
4. **Selective coding**: Identify core category; integrate all other categories around it

**Memo-writing**: Central to GT. Write analytic memos throughout to develop theoretical ideas, track decision-making, and develop the emerging theory. Memos are the bridge between coding and the final theory.

**Theoretical saturation**: Reached when new data no longer generates new theoretical insights — additional sampling adds no new theoretical properties to the core categories.

### Output

A substantive theory (often a stage model, process model, or conceptual framework) grounded in the data, expressed in a theoretical narrative supported by participant quotes.

## Interview Design

### Semi-Structured Interview Guide Structure

1. **Introduction** (5 min): Purpose, consent, recording, right to withdraw
2. **Warm-up** (5-10 min): Easy, non-threatening background questions
3. **Core questions** (30-45 min): Open questions on the central phenomenon
4. **Probing and deepening** (integrated): Follow unexpected threads
5. **Closing** (5 min): Anything not covered, debrief, next steps

### Question Types

- **Grand tour questions**: "Can you tell me about your experience with X?" — opens broad narrative
- **Mini-tour questions**: "Can you walk me through a typical day?"
- **Experience questions**: "Can you describe a specific time when..."
- **Hypothetical questions**: "If you could change one thing, what would it be?"
- **Ideal position questions**: "What would the ideal situation look like?"
- **Probing questions**: "Can you say more about that?" / "What do you mean by...?" / "How did that make you feel?"

**Avoid**: Leading questions, double-barreled questions, closed yes/no questions as main questions.

### Conducting the Interview

- Pilot test your guide with 1-2 people
- Record with participant consent (audio ± video)
- Transcribe verbatim (including pauses, false starts if relevant for discourse analysis)
- Write field notes immediately after each interview
- Memo-write about emerging ideas between interviews

### Member Checking

Show participants a summary of your analysis or key findings and ask whether this resonates with their experience. This is a primary trustworthiness strategy. Note: member checking does not "validate" your interpretation — it adds another data layer.

## Quality Criteria for Qualitative Research

Qualitative research does not use the quantitative concepts of validity and reliability. The standard alternative framework (Lincoln & Guba):

| Criterion | Definition | Strategies |
|---|---|---|
| **Credibility** | Confidence in the truth of the findings | Prolonged engagement, triangulation, member checking, peer debriefing |
| **Transferability** | Applicability to other contexts | Thick description enabling reader judgment |
| **Dependability** | Consistency of findings over time | Audit trail, methodological documentation |
| **Confirmability** | Findings are shaped by participants, not researcher bias | Reflexivity statements, audit trail |

**Reflexivity statement**: A written account of how your positionality, assumptions, and identity may have shaped the research. Required in most qualitative journals. Include in your methods section or as a supplementary statement.

Example reflexivity statement structure:
1. Who are you in relation to this topic? (insider/outsider status)
2. What assumptions did you bring to the study?
3. How did you monitor and manage your influence during data collection and analysis?
4. How might your interpretation differ from another researcher's?

## Qualitative Software Tools

### Full-Featured QDA Software

- **NVivo** (Windows/Mac): Most widely used; powerful node/case matrix features; expensive (~$700/year)
- **ATLAS.ti** (Windows/Mac): Strong for discourse and mixed methods; similar price to NVivo
- **MAXQDA** (Windows/Mac): Excellent mixed methods features; slightly more affordable

### Free Alternatives

- **Taguette** (open-source, web or desktop): Simple tag-based coding; good for small projects
- **QualCoder** (open-source, Python-based): More features than Taguette, NVivo-like interface
- **R: qualr package**: Basic qualitative coding in R
- **Dedoose** (web-based): Mixed methods focus; subscription ~$15/month

### When to use software vs paper

- Software advantage: large datasets (>20 interviews), team projects, mixed methods linking
- Paper advantage: deep single researcher engagement, phenomenological approaches emphasizing embodied reading

## Mixed Methods: Combining Qualitative and Quantitative

### When to Combine

- You need breadth (quantitative) AND depth (qualitative)
- Qualitative findings need to be tested for generalizability
- Survey results need explanation or interpretation
- Sequential exploratory: qual → quant (develop instrument from qual findings)
- Sequential explanatory: quant → qual (explain surprising quantitative results)
- Concurrent triangulation: both simultaneously, compare/merge findings

### Mixed Methods Designs

| Design | Sequence | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Exploratory sequential | Qual → Quant | Build and test instrument; generate and test theory |
| Explanatory sequential | Quant → Qual | Explain quantitative outliers or unexpected findings |
| Convergent parallel | Qual + Quant (simultaneous) | Triangulate for comprehensive understanding |
| Embedded | One method inside the other | Qualitative nested in RCT for process evaluation |

### Reporting Mixed Methods

- MMRS (Mixed Methods Reporting Standards, APA 2019)
- Specify integration point: at design, data collection, analysis, or interpretation
- Joint display: matrix or figure showing how qual and quant findings converge/diverge
